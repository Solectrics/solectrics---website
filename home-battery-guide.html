Home batteries: summer, winter & backup

A home battery can help you use electricity later — but it will not necessarily be full every evening. Here's what matters before deciding whether storage is right for your home.

A battery stores energy. It does not create it. The amount available tonight depends on how much was stored earlier, what you have already used and how the system is configured.

Three situations worth understanding

☀️
Summer sun → daytime use → possible surplus → battery

1. Sunny summer day

Longer sunny days often mean more solar generation. After the house uses what it needs, a battery may have plenty of surplus available to charge.

☁️
Winter solar → daytime heating & loads → less surplus

2. Cloudy winter day

Short days and higher home demand can leave little or no solar surplus. A battery may charge only partly, or not charge from solar that day.

🔋
Stored charge + backup equipment → selected essential circuits

3. Grid outage

With a suitable backup system, stored charge may keep selected circuits operating. Runtime depends on the charge and power demands at that moment.

These are illustrative situations, not predicted charging levels for your home. Even in summer, shade, poor weather and daytime energy use can reduce charging; clear winter days may still provide useful surplus.

What does a “9 kWh battery” actually mean?

kWh (kilowatt-hours) describes stored energy capacity. kW (kilowatts) describes the rate of power the system can supply. They are not interchangeable.

A battery advertised as 9 kWh cannot be assumed to deliver 9 kWh to the house every evening. Available energy depends on usable capacity, how full it is, charging and conversion losses, and any backup reserve.

Battery charging may also be possible from the grid on some systems. That is different from storing surplus solar, and whether it is worthwhile depends on your tariff and settings.

Common questions

Will I have enough solar to fill a battery in winter?

Not necessarily. Your solar panel size, roof orientation, shade, weather and daytime consumption all matter. A household's summer and winter bills help identify patterns, but monthly bill totals alone do not tell us how much surplus exists hour by hour.

Will a battery cover my whole evening?

Sometimes it can supply some evening demand, but this cannot be guaranteed. The usable charge available at sunset, the length of the evening and the loads in use determine how much the battery contributes before the grid is needed.

Will my solar and battery work in a power cut?

Only if the inverter, battery and installation are designed for backup. Essential circuits may need to be selected, and system power limits and an energy reserve must be considered. Solar panels alone generally shut down when the grid is down.

Should I buy a battery straight away?

Not always. Smart timing, hot-water controls and a battery-ready solar design may be sensible first steps. A home assessment can compare your priorities — savings, resilience or greater self-consumption — before recommending equipment.
